# Does an affirmative answer survive a change in quantifier?

Prospective experiment, November 24, 2026. The [predecessor](https://github.com/nousentllc/riemann-hypothesis/tree/e7a01b2d19e61e77b97ece9f1b48d48bb761e186/research/uriel/experiments/label-first-verdict) found seven cases where the model copied opposite target labels accurately but issued a positive three-way consensus verdict. Those seven selected cases motivate the present question. None enters the new test denominator.

## Question and intervention

Produce one public keyed label report for a fresh physical pair. Fork that exact visible report into two separate model calls. One asks whether the target label is Y for at least one of the two survivors. The other asks whether it is Y for both. Both questions require a binary Y/N answer and use a common output grammar. They refer to precisely the same two IDs, evidence, original lamp readings and target predicate. Keep the shared context neutral about which quantifier will follow. Score the truth of each proposition independently. Do not place the answer to one in the context of the other. Preserve the physical table in the shared history so the final reader can repair an inaccurate label report without investigator intervention. Never correct, reformat or replace the model's submitted label map. The order of the two calls is counterbalanced across records, including within ordered label pattern. The two child calls share a decoding seed for each record.

Six new circuit layouts have distinct graph vertex/edge counts relative to one another and all 32 preceding designs. Use newly assigned row IDs disjoint from their 256 older IDs. Each layout contributes four records with ordered true labels YY, NN, YN and NY once each. Two lamp observations jointly select exactly two admissible states. Taken alone, either lamp admits both target signs. Each graph is calibrated using exact rational nodal equations and an independent spanning-forest calculation. The model sees both public row labels. It is asked to copy each survivor's label under its ID before the questions are issued.

## Registered endpoints

In the order (at least one, both), the expected binary pairs are (Y,Y) for YY, (N,N) for NN, and (Y,N) for each mixed orientation. The primary endpoint is joint delivery of (Y,N) among all twelve physically mixed records. A missing checkpoint gates off both questions. A missing child counts as missing for that branch and prevents a completed joint answer. Always attempt the other authorized child after a valid checkpoint, even if the first child fails. Keep every scheduled record in the denominator, with no resampling. Report the four complete mixed signatures and incomplete pairs. Keep separate reports for inaccurate checkpoints and for available single branches. Publish case-level omissions and named counterexamples. Compute the attainable lower and upper number of mixed separations over missing binary outcomes, respecting any observed sibling answer.

On unanimous controls report the number of jointly warranted pairs and whether the model answers yes on both questions even for two negative labels. Show branches separately, mixed orientation, dispatch order, and the layout-by-layout four-record truth table. Physical truth takes precedence over the submitted map. Evaluate consistency with the submitted map separately. Report at least-one and both accuracy on all 24 opportunities, joint accuracy on all 24, transcriptions, finish reasons and actual cost. The twelve mixed records share six layouts and two children within a record share a genuine checkpoint. They are not independent samples of unseen models or tasks.

## Discriminating predictions

H1. Explicit binary wording elicits a true scope distinction. On accurately transcribed mixed pairs, the existential question gets Y and the universal question gets N. A single separated pair refutes deterministic indiscriminate substitution for every case on this panel. Good behavior on YY and NN alone provides no evidence of a distinction.

H2. Both questions follow the positive-existence rule on mixed pairs. The signature is (Y,Y), with (Y,Y) on YY and (N,N) on NN. Report this descriptive prediction and its failures, including whole-layout fits when all four mappings are present. An always-yes policy can agree with it on mixed rows and YY but diverges on NN.

H3. Other responses or transcription/delivery failures can explain the observations. Calling both questions universal predicts (N,N) on mixed pairs; reversing the two scopes predicts (N,Y). Copying one signed row can give a different pattern on YN and NY. Exact samples of these outputs do not uniquely identify a private algorithm. Both an existence rule and certain other rules have the same outputs on a restricted domain. A true judgment about at least one row and a true universal judgment are distinguishable elementary events; no assumption about the model's internal interpretation is needed to score them.

The tested contract, binary output format and fresh layouts differ from the precursor's Y/N/U consensus question and witness-ID output. A historical behavior cannot be causally assigned to a change of one word from this cross-study contrast. Within the present panel the only intended experimental change between sibling requests is their quantifier wording.
